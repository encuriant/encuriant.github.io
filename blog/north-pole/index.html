Polar-izing words and taboo
1 min read
Published on Jun 24, 2024
Updated on Aug 31, 2026

Anchored at the North Star up above the world so high, twinkles the Little Dipper not once but twice, once as a dog, once as a bear, giving the dictionary, words a pair.

(end rhyme, begin prose)

To some, the Little Dipper appeared as the dog’s tail, Kunósoura—a Greek name that became synonymous with the North Pole itself—thus finding spotlight in the word cynosure meaning the focal point of (everyone’s) attention.The “cyno” should remind us of the Latin-origin “canine”.

To others, the Little Dipper appeared as the bear of Ursa Minor, then double-dipping into the dictionary for a second word, the North Pole, the Arctic—“Arktos” being Greek for bear.

Now, why did the bear bear various sounds to our Indo-European forebears? Why ursa, arktus, medvěd (in Slavic languages, “the honey-eater”)“Med” means honey, just as in “mead” or as in the Sanskrit “madhu”.“Medvedev” means the son of the bear. and well, bear (“the brown one”)?so a polar bear… isn’t really a bear?

Apparently, this may be a linguistic phenomenon called taboo deformation where euphemisms were invented to avoid calling a thing by its name, out of a superstitious fear of invoking ita similar phenomenon is suggested of wolf, lupus (as in “lupine”) or lyco (as in “lycanthrope”)or out of politness (what the heck, oh shoot).

Had we called the polar bear “the Arctic bear” (like “the Arctic wolf”), it may have been the chai tea of the animal kingdom: not only because it is made up of two words that share an identical meaning,In the specific context of chai tea, “chai” and “tea” mean distinct things: tea is a noun referring to a class of objects, and chai is an adjective that singles out a member of that class. They do have a sense by which they become synonyms in other contexts. but also because it is a whiter version of the single-word counterpart.
